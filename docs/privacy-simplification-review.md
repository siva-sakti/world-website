# Fresh-eyes review: "flexibility without complexity — the simplest path"
*(2026-09-05, an independent agent's report, written for the owner directly. Commissioned at her ask; it decides nothing. One term: a **spot** = one bit's seat on one board — the app keeps one row per bit-per-board; the hide mark lives there.)*

## Part 1 — The honest complexity audit
The good news first: **the complexity is pay-as-you-go.** The person who never publishes meets zero privacy UI. Someone publishing all-public things meets two ideas (the board switch, the review screen). Only the mixed-life person ever meets the third switch. The structure charges people in proportion to the flexibility they use — rare, and the model's strongest property.

The stumbles — **the static structure is learnable; every real confusion lives in TIME:**
1. **The forgotten third switch meets the later flip.** March: drop a private bit on published Reading; the app marks the spot; grey; move on. June: flip the bit public from its own page — it still doesn't show on Reading. The app explains, but you're meeting a switch you didn't set by hand. **The safety and the confusion are the same mechanism**; only the ever-present "visible on: …" line pays it down.
2. **The switch nobody set.** Auto-hide-at-drop is the ONE place the app writes a switch for you. Fine as the only exception; it must stay the only one.
3. **The homeless switch.** Bits and boards have pages where their switch lives; the spot doesn't — grey on the board and the bit's "visible on" line are its only faces. A switch with no home is the easiest to forget you own.
4. **The returning bit's old hide.** Re-place a bit months later: it arrives grey — "I never hid this… oh, I did, in March." Checked: the keep-the-hide lean is CORRECT — clearing on return could silently widen what visitors see.
5. **⭐ A hole the session hadn't caught: the two-key promise is PATH-DEPENDENT.** Drop-path: the hide births with the spot → two keys. **Publish-path: private bits sitting on a private board that later publishes get NO spot-hide** (publish touches no contents' switches — correctly) → absent, nothing leaks, but each is **one flip from exposure**, guarded by a disclosure line only. Same end-state, two protection levels. **The patch: the publish review OFFERS (never auto-does) "mark these private bits' spots hidden."** One checkbox restores symmetry without breaking any rule.

Two smaller flags: **the public-copy button** in the drop moment smuggles the entire copy concept into a privacy prompt — costs more model than it saves. **The dial** adds a fourth concept + a settings schema that doesn't exist, for a resident (the mostly-public gardener) who isn't here yet.

For honesty under every option: existing bits still carry the founding-era `public` flag — **the one-time flip to private ships before any publish act does, under every model, no exceptions.**

## Part 2 — The options

### Option 1 — the current model, minus two pieces *(the recommendation)*
1. Every thing: one switch, public|private. **Born private, everyone, always. No dial.**
2. Every board/piece: same switch; publishing = flipping it through the review screen.
3. A spot can carry "not shown here" — visitors skip it; you see grey.
4. Visitors see a thing only where every switch says yes (ANDs, forever).
5. Dropping a private bit on a published board marks the spot; the moment offers **make public / leave it** — two options, no copy button.
**Mental model:** *"A visitor sees a thing only where all three switches say yes — the thing's, the board's, the spot's — and no act ever flips a switch you didn't touch."*
**Costs:** three concepts; the timeline stumbles (mitigated by the "visible on" line, never removed). The dial's removal costs the owner nothing — born-private + the review's bulk-flip IS the no-labor default; the dial is safely additive the day a gardener-resident is real.
**Saves:** the settings schema · the fourth concept · the forgotten-dial danger · the copy machinery.
**Desires check: all eight hold.** The bulk lever survives AND respects hides — a board-wide "make everything public" never touches a hidden spot; the mentor bits stay grey through a bulk sweep. *(The single best argument for the spot-hide.)*

### Option 2 — two switches only (the session's own N22 shape)
Things public|private, born private · boards/pieces same + review · visitor sees public-on-public · every visibility-changing act speaks · that's all.
**Mental model:** *"A visitor sees a thing only where a public thing sits on a public board — and the app tells you whenever that's about to change."*
**What it loses, exactly:** the mentor scenario SURVIVES (private bits are absent for the visitor, visible to her). What's lost is **the two-key property**: the mentor bit is ONE act from exposure — including inside a bulk sweep with aggregated disclosure, skimmed. "No accidental exposure ever" downgrades from *structurally impossible* to *disclosed at the moment*. Also lost: shown-here-not-there arrangements (fallback: take it off the board).
**Saves:** a whole concept, the grey, the birth rule, the homeless switch, timeline stumbles 1–4 entirely. The simplest model that keeps most of the list.

### Option 3 — board-only visibility (and the Are.na single-switch cousin) — **REJECTED**
Bits have no switch; boards do; hide per spot (or not even that).
**Why it dies:** the global "this morning page is mine alone, everywhere" stops existing; safety becomes per-place labor forever; one hurried drop exposes; the review becomes forced triage of every card; a published piece windowing a raw bit exposes it. Are.na gets away with it because connecting-to-a-public-channel MEANS publishing there — **here, placing is thinking, not publishing; the same bit on many boards is the core move.** Overloading placement with publishing breaks the product's own physics.

## Part 3 — The timeline cases, stressed
| case | Option 1 | Option 2 | Option 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| private bit dropped on published board | spot marked, grey at once — safe and legible | absent, marked in your view — safe | **visible immediately** unless hidden — the trap |
| bit flipped public months later | still hidden there; the moment says where it still won't show | **exposed in that one act** (bulk sweeps too) | no bit switch exists |
| board published later, mixed contents | review lists what won't show — **but no second key on this path**: the Part-1 patch | same review; disclosure-only but consistently so | forced triage of every card |
| re-placed bit, old hide | returns grey; correct | nothing to stress | returns grey |

**Consistency note:** pieces already run Option 2 (no per-window hide inside compositions — ruled N25); the spot-hide is a **boards-only** concept. Legitimate — boards are where arrangements are shared — but it should be *chosen out loud*, not drifted into.

## Part 4 — Recommendation, and the one question
**Option 1: the current model minus the dial and minus the public-copy offer, plus one decision on the publish-path patch.** No simpler structure keeps the whole list — every genuine simplification pays with the same coin: "no accidental exposure ever" weakens from a property of the structure to a property of a moment. The spot-hide isn't there for the mentor scenario (one switch covers that); **it's there because it's the only mechanism that makes exposure take two acts DURABLY** — surviving a forgotten setting, a later flip, and a bulk sweep. The dial and copy-offer pull no v1 weight; defer both. Spend the one big UI investment on **the "visible on: … (hidden there / board private)" line on every bit** — it converts the three-owner structure from memorized into answered.

> **THE ONE QUESTION: when you say "no accidental exposure ever" — must that be guaranteed by the STRUCTURE (a second stored switch, so even a bulk flip or a forgotten setting cannot expose the mentor's board), or is a clear SENTENCE at the moment of the act enough?**
> Structure → Option 1, and then yes/no on the publish-review offering hides so the guarantee holds on both paths. Sentence → Option 2 — the simplest model on the table, the shape you yourself converged on once, with everything given up contained in the word "ever."
